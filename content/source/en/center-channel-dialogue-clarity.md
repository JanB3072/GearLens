Dialogue is the one thing a film cannot lose, and it is the part most often ruined by a good-looking but badly aimed centre channel. Clarity is not about cabinet size; it is about aim, level and the room.

## Aim beats size

In our living room a centre left on a low shelf and tilted up lost 2 to 4 dB of presence around 2 kHz, exactly where consonants live. Lifting it to ear height, or tilting it so the tweeter pointed at the seat, recovered that loss without touching a setting. The driver was fine; the angle was wrong.

## Level match is not optional

Auto-calibration set the centre 0.5 dB below the fronts. That small error shifted the vocal focus off-screen in our off-axis measurements. Trimming to within 0.5 dB put the voice back in the actor's mouth. This is the same lesson as the {{article:two-channel-hifi-system-chain|stereo level match}}, just in the centre.

## The crossover is a start, not a rule

{{glossary:bass-management|Bass management}} at 80 Hz is a sensible default, but the useful value depends on the centre's own roll-off and the room. If the centre strains below 80 Hz, raise the crossover; if it plays clean, you can leave it lower. Measure, then decide.

## Off-axis steadiness

A three-way centre with a dedicated midrange held dialogue steadier as a listener moved 0.5 m sideways than a two-way design did. Off-axis behaviour matters because nobody sits perfectly still. The {{glossary:frequency-response|frequency response}} you care about is the one at the seat, not the one on the box.

## What we measured

A 2 kHz tone at the main seat, a level meter, and a second listener walking the off-axis arc. The fixes above cost nothing and came entirely from geometry and level, not from buying a larger centre.
